The case for shoulder season

You're arriving at the exact hinge of the year.

Early October is the sweet spot the Turkish coast quietly saves for people who don't need to travel in school-holiday July or August. The sea has spent five months absorbing heat and is still around 25–26°C in October, just as the air finally cools enough to make a 15 km mountain day pleasant rather than punishing.

Bring one layer for evenings in the mountains — Tahtalı's summit station sits at 2,365 m and can be 15–20°C cooler than the beach — and a light rain shell; early October occasionally sees the season's first short showers roll in off the Mediterranean.

Every ruin here has a story attached

You are walking through Lycia, not just Turkey.

Before this was Antalya, this coast was part of Lycia — a federation of city-states whose larger cities had three votes, middle-sized cities two and smaller cities one. Hamilton cited that system in Federalist No. 9. The Lycian Way, the long-distance trail this trip dips into, threads directly between their ruins.

The Chimera's breath

Yanartaş means "burning stone." The Greeks believed a fire-breathing hybrid beast — lion, goat and serpent — lived in this mountainside, slain here by the hero Bellerophon on his winged horse Pegasus. The flames are real: methane and hydrogen seep through fractured rock and have burned, unfed, for at least 2,500 years. Pliny the Elder wrote about them.

The city Alexander skipped

Termessos sits so high and so defensible that Alexander the Great failed to force the issue in 333 BC and moved on rather than spend the campaign on it. It's one of the only Anatolian cities he never took — which is why it still looks half-abandoned rather than rebuilt by whoever conquered it next.

A king's name on a city

Antalya was founded around 150 BC by Attalus II of Pergamon, who reportedly sent scouts to find "heaven on earth" and settled on this bay. He named it Attaleia. Two thousand years of Turkish sound-shift later, you get Antalya.

Rabbit holes before you go.

None of us knows this coast yet, so treat these as launch points rather than homework. The useful trick is that the mythology, archaeology and walking geography all overlap: the same names keep reappearing for very old reasons.

⚓

Phaselis: three harbours, 2,700 years

Founded by Rhodians around 690 BC, Phaselis became the principal commercial port on this stretch of coast. Alexander the Great was here in 333 BC; later the pirate Zeniketes controlled it before Rome intervened.

Yanartaş: geology became a monster

Dozens of gas vents burn continuously above Çıralı. A ruined Temple of Hephaestus sits below them; Pliny described the mountain as burning day and night, and the glow was a natural landmark to ancient mariners.

Olympos: league heavyweight → pirate stronghold

Olympos once had three votes in the Lycian League — the maximum. In the first century BC it became associated with Zeniketes and piracy; when Roman forces closed in, the pirate ruler reportedly burned himself and his family rather than surrender.

The Lycian League: ancient weighted representation

Twenty-three city-states shared a federal council, with one, two or three votes depending on size. The system impressed Montesquieu, and Hamilton explicitly invoked Lycia in Federalist No. 9 while arguing about the US Constitution.

Tahtalı: the other Olympus

In antiquity this mountain was the Lycian Olympus. A temple of Hephaestus stood on it, the Lycian Way crosses it, and the modern cable car gains 1,639 vertical metres in about ten minutes.

Termessos: the unexcavated mountain city

Built around 1,000 m up in Pisidia, Termessos resisted Alexander's advance. Remarkably, it has never been formally excavated: the forested ruins remain in situ, while scholars have recorded more than a thousand inscriptions.

Çıralı: beach village in an archaeological corridor

Çıralı is only a small agricultural village, but a 3.5 km protected beach connects it on foot to Olympos while Yanartaş burns in the hills behind. It is almost absurdly well positioned for a low-effort family day.

Göynük: easy gorge, hard mountains above

The canyon sits directly on the Lycian Way. The lower gorge has pools and waterfalls; keep following the long-distance trail and the terrain quickly turns into the much rougher Beydağları mountain stage.

Preferred base

Çamyuva / Kiriş

This is the deliberate compromise pin on the map: far enough from central Kemer to keep pine forest at your doorstep, close enough that you're never more than fifteen minutes from a proper restaurant. Çamyuva itself grew up around a stream mouth and still has more trees than concrete compared to Kemer proper — Kiriş sits a little further north, trading a few minutes of drive time toward Phaselis for a few minutes saved toward Antalya and Göynük.

A hire car is doing real work on this trip. Public transport (the dolmuş minibus network) covers the coast road, but it won't get you to a trailhead at 8am or back from a sunset at Yanartaş.

Nine full days · weather gets the final vote

A rhythm, not a checklist.

Big walking days are bracketed by slack days on purpose — the fastest way to ruin a family holiday is to stack ambition back-to-back. Anything below can move if the forecast, the four-year-old, or plain inclination disagrees. The two big hiking days are adult-led split-party days; the rest work as whole-family outings or easy swaps.

Eat like you're actually in Antalya

The regional table, not just "Turkish food."

Antalya Province has its own specialities distinct from Istanbul or the Aegean coast — mostly built around citrus, pine honey and cold-water trout from the mountain streams you'll be walking beside.

🥗

Antalya piyazı

A local white-bean salad with tahini and vinegar dressing — nothing like the piyaz found elsewhere in Turkey.

🐟

Alabalık (trout)

Farmed in the cold streams around Ulupınar and Göynük and grilled streamside — the classic Lycian Way lunch stop.

🫓

Gözleme & tantuni

Village-style stuffed flatbread and Mersin-style spiced diced meat wraps — reliable, fast, and everywhere along the coast road.

🍊

Citrus & pomegranate

October is deep harvest season for both. Fresh-squeezed juice stands are worth the stop, and pomegranate turns up in everything from salad to şıra.

To drink: şıra (fresh unfermented grape juice, autumn-only), ayran after a hot walk, and çay (tea) served constantly, in small tulip glasses, usually free with a meal. Rakı, an aniseed spirit taken with meze and ice water, is the classic evening drink if you want the full local ritual.

The boring bits, handled once

Practical toolkit.

Money

Turkish lira (TRY)

  • Card accepted almost everywhere; carry some cash for village stalls and canyon/trailhead parking
  • Round up or add 5–10% for restaurant service; not expected in shops or taxis
Driving

Hire car essentials

  • UK/EU licence valid; carry it plus passport in the car at all times
  • Tolls (otoyol) are camera-based — confirm the hire company's toll billing before you drive one
  • Mountain village roads narrow fast; a compact car earns its keep
Connectivity

SIM & power

  • Turkcell/Vodafone tourist e-SIMs cover the whole route, including most trailheads
  • Type C/F two-pin sockets, 230V — same as continental Europe
A little Turkish

Goes a long way

  • Merhaba — hello · Teşekkürler — thank you
  • Afiyet olsun — said to you before/after eating, worth returning
  • Nerede...? — "where is...?"

Pack light, pack right

What earns its space in the case.

For the mountains

  • Light waterproof / windproof layer for Tahtalı's summit
  • Proper trail shoes — limestone gets polished and slippery
  • 2L+ water capacity; refill points are sparse above the treeline

For the sea

  • Water shoes for pebble beaches and Göynük Canyon
  • Reef-safe sunscreen — several coves are marine-protected
  • Dry bag for phones on any boat or canyon day

Everything else

  • Headtorch for a dusk descent from Yanartaş
  • Cash in small notes for canyon/trailhead parking
  • A modest cover-up for mosque visits in Antalya old town
